# Methods review: Hicks (1935), "The Theory of Monopoly"

- **Paper:** Hicks, J. R. (1935). "Annual Survey of Economic Theory: The Theory of Monopoly." *Econometrica* 3(1),
  January 1935, pp. 1–20. Author's affiliation as printed at the end of the article: London School of Economics.
- **Statistical focus:** The "quiet life" claim in section I.5 (p. 8), that a monopolist facing rising subjective
  costs of effort will not bother to get close to maximum profit because profit varies little near its peak, and
  the formal results it leans on (the marginal revenue equals marginal cost condition and its second-order
  condition, pp. 3–4).
- **Review standard:** Internal consistency of the formal results, explicit statement of assumptions, and the
  testability of the predictions. Empirical reporting standards (sampling, uncertainty, data availability) are noted
  where they would apply and marked as not applicable.
- **What was read:** The full text of the article, pp. 1–20, in the scan of *Econometrica* volume 3 (1935) held by
  the Internet Archive (item `sim_econometrica_1935_3`), including footnotes. The volume's erratum notice (for a
  different article) was checked. Secondary sources consulted for context only: the abstracts of Leibenstein (1966),
  Bertrand and Mullainathan (2003), Giroud and Mueller (2010), Syverson (2004) and Perelman (2011). Several
  equations in the scan are garbled by optical character recognition; where that matters it is flagged below.

## Methods summary

### Research design

A survey of recent theoretical work on monopoly, organized under three headings: simple monopoly (pp. 2–8),
monopolistic competition and duopoly (pp. 8–16) and bilateral monopoly (pp. 16–18), followed by a conclusion on
applicability (pp. 18–20). The method is deductive. Hicks restates results of Cournot, Robinson, Chamberlin,
von Stackelberg, Zeuthen, Schneider, Harrod and Bowley in calculus, adds his own interpretations, and in places
offers original arguments (the private-factor argument of section I.5, the restatement of bilateral monopoly with
marginal revenue curves, and the conjectural-variations synthesis credited partly to unpublished work by W. M.
Allen, footnote 25).

### Sampling strategy

Not applicable. There is no sample of firms, industries or observations. The "sample" is the literature surveyed,
and the selection is stated explicitly (the four books named on p. 1 and journal articles cited in footnotes 2–40).
No search or inclusion protocol for the literature is described, which is normal for a 1935 survey.

### Sample size

Not applicable. No data are analyzed.

### Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For the literature, Hicks confines the survey to monopoly theory (p. 1) and gives his reason (the volume of recent
work). Within simple monopoly he notes that most writers assume a single product and criticizes that restriction
(p. 6). No exclusion rules for data apply.

### Independent and dependent variables

None in the empirical sense. The formal objects are output $`x`$, price $`p = f(x)`$, total cost $`\phi(x)`$,
factor quantities and prices, demand elasticity, and conjectural variations $`\partial x_2/\partial x_1`$. In the
quiet-life passage the implicit outcome is how close the monopolist's output is to the profit-maximizing output, and
the implicit driver is the curvature of profit near its maximum together with the rate at which the monopolist's
subjective costs rise.

### Statistical methods

None. The "methods" are first- and second-order conditions for maximization, comparative statics, and geometric
arguments (the tangency solution, reaction curves, the bilateral monopoly diagram on p. 17).

### Reported effect sizes

None. The quiet-life passage is qualitative. It states that the variation in profit near the maximum may often be
small and depends on the difference between the slopes of the marginal revenue and marginal cost curves (p. 8,
citing Bowley), but gives no magnitude, example or calibration.

### Confidence intervals

Not applicable. No estimates are reported.

### P-values

Not applicable.

### Missing data treatment

Not applicable.

### Stated limitations

Hicks states several. Introducing the monopolist's own effort ("private factors") requires either constant
marginal utility of money or Paretian indifference curves, which he calls cumbrous and less informative (p. 8). He
rates as more serious still the second difficulty, that monopoly effort may go into finding where to restrict output
rather than into output (p. 8). In the conclusion (pp. 18–20) he is skeptical that monopoly theory can be
applied to practical problems, because the demand curve facing a firm is a subjective estimate, and because the
expansion of a firm is stopped by subjective estimates under both perfect and imperfect competition, so that
objective facts cannot distinguish the two cases (p. 19).

## Interpretation notes

- The quiet-life sentence is a conjecture appended to a formal argument, not a derived result. Two ingredients are
  stated (flat profit near the peak and sharply rising subjective costs), but the second is offered as a likely possibility
  (p. 8), not shown.
- The argument is about the monopolist's own effort in finding the profit-maximizing output. Later uses, from
  Leibenstein's X-inefficiency to Bertrand and Mullainathan's managerial-preferences study, extend it to production
  inefficiency, wages and plant decisions. Those extensions go beyond what the 1935 text says.
- The flatness claim is a standard second-order property and is internally consistent. With linear demand and
  constant marginal cost, missing the best output by a fraction $`d`$ costs a share $`d^2`$ of maximum profit, a
  precise form of the statement Hicks attributes to Bowley.
- Hicks's own conclusion (p. 19) limits testability. If the stopping point of a firm is set by subjective estimates
  in both competitive and monopolistic settings, outside observers cannot read slack directly off behavior. Any
  empirical test has to use indirect consequences, such as productivity dispersion or growth.
- Reporting issues, kept separate from errors: no numerical example accompanies the quiet-life claim, and the
  conditions under which slack would be large are not stated. No published correction or comment documenting an
  analytical error in this article was found; the erratum in the same volume concerns a different paper.
- The modern rerun in this repository does not reproduce anything in the paper, since the paper has no data. It
  takes the conjecture's most direct industry-level implications (wider within-industry productivity dispersion and
  slower growth where concentration is high) and checks them on 2002–2023 US manufacturing data. It finds wider
  output-per-hour dispersion in concentrated industries, little difference in TFP dispersion once tobacco is set
  aside, no cross-sectional difference in growth, and narrowing TFP dispersion where concentration rose.

## Unanswered questions

- How large is the profit loss from slack relative to the effort saved, and for which demand and cost shapes is it
  big enough to matter? The paper gives no calibration.
- Does the argument apply to managers of corporations, whose subjective costs are not the owners', or only to
  owner-monopolists? The text speaks of the monopolist and of people in monopoly positions.
- Through what channel would competition discipline the slack: entry, takeover, product-market share loss? The
  paper does not say.
- Is slack taken in output choice only, or also in costs? The passage concerns getting close to the profit-
  maximizing output; whether costs themselves are higher is left open.

## Items requiring expert verification
